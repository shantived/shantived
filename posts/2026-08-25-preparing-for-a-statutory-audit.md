A statutory audit goes smoothly when the groundwork is done before the audit team walks in. Most of the delays, queries and last-minute stress we see come from the same handful of gaps, and every one of them can be closed in advance. Here is the checklist we share with clients ahead of year-end.

## 1. Close the books properly

The trial balance should be final before the audit begins, not "final for now". That means:

- All bank accounts reconciled to the last day of the year, with every reconciling item explained.
- Cash on hand physically counted and matched to the cash book.
- Sales and purchases cut off cleanly at the year-end date, so that goods delivered or received after the year end are not booked in the wrong year.
- Provisions made for expenses that belong to the year but were billed later (audit fees, utilities, professional charges, bonuses).

## 2. Reconcile the tax returns with the books

Auditors will compare what the business reported to the tax authorities with what the books say. Reconcile these yourself first:

- GST returns against the sales and purchase registers, including credit notes and reversals.
- Tax deducted at source against the expense ledgers and the challans paid.
- Advance tax and self-assessment tax against the tax ledger.

A reconciliation prepared by the business, with differences explained, saves days of back-and-forth.

## 3. Keep the registers and schedules ready

Have these prepared as clean schedules, ideally in the same format every year:

- Fixed asset register with additions, disposals, depreciation and the physical location of major assets.
- Ageing of receivables and payables, with a note on doubtful balances.
- Inventory valuation working, with the basis of valuation stated.
- Loans and borrowings, with sanction letters, repayment schedules and year-end confirmations.
- Related-party list and all transactions with them during the year.

## 4. Gather the supporting documents

The audit team will ask for documents behind the numbers. The usual set includes bank statements for every account, loan statements, major contracts and agreements, invoices for large purchases and sales, board or partner resolutions, and copies of all statutory filings made during the year. Keeping these in one indexed folder, physical or digital, is the single biggest time-saver.

## 5. Review last year's points

Every audit ends with observations and suggestions. Before the next audit begins, go through the previous year's points and record what was done about each. Auditors follow up on these, and a business that has acted on them starts the engagement on strong footing.

## 6. Nominate one point of contact

Queries flow faster when one person in the business owns the audit: someone who knows where the records are, who can answer questions or find the person who can, and who tracks open items. Without this, the same question travels through three people and takes a week to answer.

## The payoff

A well-prepared audit is not only quicker. It produces better financial statements, fewer qualifications, and a clearer picture of the business for its owners, lenders and partners. Preparation is the cheapest part of the whole exercise, and it is entirely within the business's control.
