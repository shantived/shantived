In a small business, control is personal. The owner signs every cheque, sees every invoice and knows every customer. That works until it does not: the day the business has more transactions than one person can watch, the informal system quietly stops protecting anyone.

Internal controls are the habits and rules that replace that personal oversight with something that scales. They are not bureaucracy. Done well, they are invisible most of the time and invaluable the one time something goes wrong.

## What internal controls actually do

A control is any step that prevents an error or irregularity, or catches it quickly. Most useful controls fall into four groups:

- **Authorisation**: who may commit the business to spending, and up to what amount.
- **Segregation of duties**: the person who records a transaction is not the one who approves it or handles the money.
- **Reconciliation**: independent records are compared regularly, and differences are chased until explained.
- **Physical and access control**: cash, stock, cheque books, passwords and signing authority are restricted to the people who need them.

## The controls we recommend first

If a business has none of this formally in place, these five give the most protection for the least effort:

1. **A spending authority matrix.** A one-page table stating who can approve what, with limits. It ends the ambiguity that fraud and waste both rely on.
2. **Monthly bank reconciliation by someone other than the person making payments.** This single control catches a remarkable share of problems.
3. **Two people on every payment above a threshold.** One prepares, one approves. Most banking platforms support this natively.
4. **A vendor master that only one person can change.** New vendors and changed bank details are where payment fraud usually enters.
5. **A monthly management review.** Owners or partners look at a short pack: cash position, receivables ageing, unusual expenses, and any exceptions flagged during the month.

## Signs the controls are weak

Some patterns are worth noticing early: reconciliations that are always "in progress", one employee who never takes leave and handles everything financial, vendor bank details that change without a paper trail, and expenses that are approved after they have been paid. None of these proves a problem. Each of them removes the ability to know.

## Growing without losing control

Controls should grow with the business, not ahead of it. A ten-person firm does not need the manual of a listed company. It needs a few clear rules, applied consistently, reviewed once a year, and tightened as the numbers grow. The goal is simple: the owners should be able to trust the numbers because the system produces them reliably, not because they personally checked every one.
